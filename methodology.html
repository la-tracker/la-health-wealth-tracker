How the scores are built

Methodology and data note

Built by Zoe White · Version 2.2, prototype · September 2026

What this tool does

The LA Health & Wealth Tracker is an interactive map of Los Angeles. It shows how money, health, and neighborhood resources line up across the city's 15 City Council districts.

Click any district and you get three scores plus one combined score. The map colors each district by whichever score you choose. Green marks better conditions. Red marks worse ones.

The idea behind the tool is straightforward. Where a person lives in Los Angeles is closely tied to how long that person is likely to live. This map makes that connection easier to see.

Read this before you read the scores

The scores in this version are estimates, not verified counts.

They were built to show how the tool works and what it could do once full data is loaded. They follow the direction of real public data, and they have not been checked against complete district-level records yet.

A verified version is in progress. Treat every number here as a demonstration. Before quoting any figure somewhere else, read the section on what this tool cannot tell you.

The three scores

Each district is measured three ways. Three, not four. A section further down explains why policy is not a fourth score.

ScoreWhat it measuresWhat goes into it
Financial and Community Resources The money and institutions a typical resident can draw on Median household income, homeownership rate, school ratings, park and nature access
Health Risk How common poor health outcomes are in the district Substance use rates, chronic disease rates, life expectancy
Neighborhood Access How easy it is to reach the everyday things a neighborhood needs Healthy food retail, banking and lending services, parks and recreation

How a score is built

Every score runs from 0 to 100. Across the 15 districts, 100 marks the best conditions and 0 marks the worst. The scores compare districts to each other, so a 100 means best in Los Angeles rather than best in the country.

Building a score takes three steps.

Step 1. Put every measure on the same scale

Each score is built from several measures, and those measures use different units. Income is counted in dollars. Life expectancy is counted in years. Asthma is counted as a percentage. Dollars and years cannot be averaged together, so every measure gets converted to a 0 to 100 score first.

The district with the best value for a measure gets 100. The district with the worst value gets 0. Every other district lands in between, based on how far it sits from each end.

Score = (district value − lowest value) ÷ (highest value − lowest value) × 100

Here is an example. Median household income across the 15 districts runs from about $38,000 in the lowest district to about $112,000 in the highest. A district at $75,000 lands close to the middle.

(75,000 − 38,000) ÷ (112,000 − 38,000) × 100 = 50

Some measures run the other way, because a high number is a bad sign. A high asthma rate is worse than a low one. Those measures get flipped, so that 100 always stands for the best result.

Flipped score = 100 − score

Step 2. Average the measures inside each score

Once every measure inside a score sits on the 0 to 100 scale, they are averaged together.

Score = (measure 1 + measure 2 + … + measure N) ÷ N

In this version every measure inside a score counts the same amount. Later versions plan to weight some measures more heavily than others, based on how strongly published research ties them to health and financial security.

Step 3. Combine the three scores into one

The combined score shown on the map is an average of the three.

Combined score = (Resources + (100 − Health Risk) + Neighborhood Access) ÷ 3

Health Risk gets subtracted from 100 before the average is taken. The next section explains why.

A note on the Health Risk score

Health Risk runs in the opposite direction from the other two scores. A score of 0 means the lowest risk in the city. A score of 100 means the highest risk. Here, a low number is the good result.

Two things follow from that.

  • On the map, the Health Risk colors are flipped, so green still marks the healthier districts.
  • In the combined score, Health Risk is subtracted from 100 first, so all three scores point the same direction before they are averaged.

The district panel labels this score as risk, and the map legend says which end is which, so the direction stays clear wherever the number appears.

Where the data comes from

The scoring structure is built so that verified data can be dropped straight into it. These are the sources the finished version draws on.

  • LA County Department of Public Health. Chronic disease rates and life expectancy by community, from the Community Health Needs Assessment.
  • USC Price School, Neighborhood Data for Social Change. Income, homeownership, school quality, and population data by neighborhood.
  • USDA Food Environment Atlas. Food access and healthy food retail.
  • LA City Planning Department. Park access, green space, and zoning.
  • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Lending patterns and access to financial services.
  • California Department of Education. School performance ratings.

The policy snapshots, and why they are not scored

Each district panel carries a short summary of recent policy activity. Those notes come from city council records, Metro, the LA Housing Department, and state agencies, and they cover 2025 and 2026.

They are written as sentences, not turned into a score. Nothing in a policy snapshot feeds any index. That is a deliberate choice, and the reasoning is worth setting out, because the obvious question about this tracker is why policy is not the fourth score.

What was tested

Four candidate measures were checked to see whether each one could be sourced for all 15 districts, from one consistent source, with one consistent definition. Three of the four failed.

  • Right to Counsel coverage. The published priority list covers the whole county, including cities that are not Los Angeles. ZIP codes also do not line up with council district lines, so any district number would depend on which mapping rule someone picked, and different rules change which districts look covered.
  • Counts of policy designations. A wildfire hazard zone and a food desert designation would each score one point while pointing in opposite directions, since one tends to fall on wealthier hillside areas and the other on lower-income ones. Several of these designations are also built from the same underlying data, so counting them separately adds confidence without adding information.
  • Capital dollars per resident. City budget records are not organized by district. In the one dataset that does carry a district field, about 40 percent of the spending has no district recorded. Council districts are also drawn to hold nearly equal populations, so dividing by population does almost nothing.

The fourth measure, households that received eviction defense representation, could be sourced for all 15 districts. It was set aside anyway, because about a third of its district values are estimated rather than counted, it covers one legal provider rather than all of them, and it was published once with no sign it will be published again.

The structural reason

Most Los Angeles housing and tenant policy applies citywide. Rent stabilization rules, Measure ULA, and state housing law are the same in all 15 districts. A score built on citywide policy would produce the same number everywhere and would tell a reader nothing.

What does differ between districts is how much of that policy actually reaches residents. That information exists, inside the city's contracts with service providers, and the city does not publish it. Until it does, scoring policy would mean putting a number on something the data cannot carry.

So the snapshots stay as sentences. They say what happened, where, and when. A reader can weigh them against the three scores and draw their own conclusions.

One thing the snapshots do not claim

Placing policy next to conditions is not the same as showing that the policy caused the conditions. This tracker takes a single picture in time, and a single picture cannot separate cause from coincidence. The snapshots say what government did. The scores say what the district is living with. Whether one produced the other is a question this tool raises rather than answers.

What this tool cannot tell you

Five limits are worth knowing before anyone uses these scores.

  • The numbers are estimates. See the note near the top of this page.
  • One score covers a whole district. Each Los Angeles council district holds roughly 260,000 people, and conditions shift block by block inside them. A district average can hide a struggling neighborhood sitting right next to a comfortable one.
  • The scores are relative. A 100 means best among these 15 districts. It does not mean the conditions are good by any national standard.
  • The map shows conditions, not causes. A low score describes what a district is living with. It does not explain how those conditions came about, and it says nothing about the people who live there.
  • District lines change. The map uses the official city boundaries from the 2012 redistricting, because that is the version available as open data right now. The 2021 lines moved several districts, so a few neighborhoods sit in the wrong district on this map. Swapping in the 2021 boundaries is the next fix. Los Angeles is also weighing changes to how district lines get drawn and how many districts there should be.

What comes next

  • Replace the estimated scores with verified data, one index at a time.
  • Swap the 2012 boundaries for the 2021 redistricting lines.
  • Weight the measures inside each score using published research.
  • Ask the LA Housing Department for service delivery counts by council district, which is the one path to a policy score the published data does not currently support.
  • List the source and the year behind every single measure, so any number can be traced.
  • Set a schedule for updates and show the date of the last one on the map itself.

Questions about the method are welcome. This page will be revised as the data behind the tracker is verified.
